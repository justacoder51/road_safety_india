# Road Safety in India: Scale, Comparisons, Policy Effectiveness, and an Adaptation Agenda

**Research report for a future public-information website**  
**Prepared:** 27 October 2026  
**Geographic focus:** India, with comparisons to Viet Nam, Singapore, Sweden, the Netherlands, the United Kingdom, and Australia

## Executive summary

Road crashes are a large and persistent source of preventable death and disability in India. The supplied Ministry of Road Transport and Highways (MoRTH) *Road Accidents in India 2024* report records **487,707 crashes, 177,175 deaths, and 471,441 injured people** in 2024. That is about **486 deaths a day**. Two-wheelers accounted for the largest share of deaths (46.2%) and pedestrians for 20.6%, according to the report's published summary. These numbers should be presented as police-reported administrative data, not as a complete census of all road injuries.

A separate WHO estimate for 2021 is higher than India's reported count: **216,618 estimated deaths (95% CI 193,271–239,965)** and **15.4 deaths per 100,000 population**, compared with **153,972 deaths reported by India** that year. The gap is evidence of undercounting and differences in estimation and case capture; it is not evidence that one figure is a correction for 2024.

Comparisons need more than raw totals. India has a very large population and road-user mix, while Singapore has a small population, strong data systems, dense urban travel and a different network. Use the same source, year, definition and denominator whenever possible. WHO's harmonized estimates are suitable for cross-country mortality-rate comparisons; MoRTH's reports are best for India's detailed national, state and road-user breakdowns. Our World in Data's WHO-based chart is useful for modeled historical death counts, not for comparing risk per person unless paired with population denominators.

India has legislation and programmes addressing licensing, vehicle standards, helmets and seat belts, road engineering, emergency care and enforcement. The Motor Vehicles (Amendment) Act 2019 increased penalties and created new mechanisms, but national before-and-after trends cannot establish its causal effect. A 2020 parliamentary answer said the Ministry had not conducted a specific impact study. One small autopsy-based study and state-level short-term comparisons are not a substitute for a controlled national evaluation.

International examples offer transferable methods rather than plug-in solutions: Viet Nam's comprehensive helmet law paired with visible enforcement and communication; Singapore's automated enforcement and transparent reporting; Sweden's Vision Zero and safe-system governance; the Netherlands' functional road hierarchy and forgiving infrastructure; the UK's measured average-speed-camera evaluation; and Victoria, Australia's staged driver licensing. For India, the strongest package combines safe speeds, safe roads, safe vehicles, safer road users, and timely post-crash care, with state-level accountability and transparent outcome measurement.

## 1. What should the future website explain?

The website should help a visitor answer five questions:

1. How many people are killed or injured on Indian roads, and who is most exposed?
2. How do India's numbers compare with other countries when population and data definitions are considered?
3. What has India already tried, and what can the evidence say about results?
4. What have other countries done, and what part of those approaches could fit India?
5. Which interventions should be prioritized, where, by whom, and how will progress be measured?

Keep deaths and serious injuries visible together. A crash count is not a measure of harm by itself: a country can report many minor crashes because its reporting is more complete, while serious injuries can be under-recorded. The website should distinguish fatalities, serious injuries, all reported injuries, crashes, and modeled estimates.

## 2. India: scale and distribution of the problem

### 2.1 National totals and recent trend

The supplied *Road Accidents in India 2024* report attributes the following 2024 totals to state and Union Territory police departments:

| Indicator | 2024 | Plain-language interpretation |
|---|---:|---|
| Reported road crashes | 487,707 | About 1,336 per day |
| People killed | 177,175 | About 486 per day, or 20 per hour |
| People injured | 471,441 | Reported injuries; not necessarily all injuries |

The report summary states that road deaths rose about 2.5% from 2023 (172,890), while reported crashes rose about 1.5%. These rates are from police submissions and can change if state data are revised or late submissions are incorporated. The provided file is the primary reference for this report; the future site should retain its edition and release date beside headline numbers.

The longer trend shows that fatalities did not return to the pre-pandemic level after the exceptional mobility disruption in 2020. MoRTH reported 157,593 deaths in 2018, 158,984 in 2019, 138,383 in 2020, 153,972 in 2021, 168,491 in 2022, and 172,890 in 2023. The 2020 dip should not be framed as a durable safety gain: lockdowns and reduced travel materially changed exposure.

### 2.2 Who is most affected?

The 2024 report summary identifies two-wheelers as the largest road-user group among fatalities (46.2%), followed by pedestrians (20.6%) and occupants of cars, taxis, vans and light motor vehicles (12.4%). The pattern has direct policy implications:

- **Two-wheeler riders and passengers:** protect through certified, correctly fastened helmets for both rider and passenger; safer speeds; conspicuous enforcement; safer junctions; and separation from high-speed traffic where feasible.
- **Pedestrians:** provide continuous footpaths, safe crossings, refuge islands, lighting, access to transit, and slower speeds where people walk. A pedestrian's survival should not depend on flawless judgement in a high-speed traffic stream.
- **Vehicle occupants:** increase seat-belt use in every seating position, improve vehicle crashworthiness and restraint use, and manage speed and impairment.
- **Children, older people, cyclists and people with disabilities:** use universal-access design, safe school zones, protected crossings and speed management rather than relying only on education.

### 2.3 Where are deaths concentrated?

The MoRTH 2024 summary reports 150,958 crashes on National Highways including expressways (31.0% of crashes), 103,538 on State Highways (21.2%), and 233,211 on other roads (47.8%). Fatalities are distributed across all three classes: 64,772 (36.6%) on National Highways, 39,277 (22.2%) on State Highways, and 73,126 (41.3%) on other roads. This cautions against treating road safety as a highway-only issue. Urban arterials, local streets, rural roads, and the places where people cross or access transit also need treatment.

Uttar Pradesh recorded the highest state fatality total in the 2024 report summary (24,118); Tamil Nadu recorded the most crashes (67,526). Total counts help target resources but are not a fair ranking of risk without exposure measures such as population, registered vehicles, traffic volume or vehicle-kilometres. State comparisons should show both counts and rates, and identify which denominator is being used.

### 2.4 Administrative count versus modeled burden

WHO's 2023 country profile reports India's 2021 police-reported deaths as 153,972 and WHO-estimated deaths as 216,618 (95% confidence interval 193,271–239,965), with an estimated rate of 15.4 per 100,000 population. WHO's estimate is a statistical model informed by available data and is useful for international comparability; it should not be blended into a MoRTH trend line. Police-reported figures depend on reporting, definitions and follow-up. The website should show both series separately, explain what each means, and avoid implying that the WHO estimate is the actual 2024 total.

## 3. International comparison: compare like with like

### 3.1 Recommended comparison method

Use WHO's *Global Status Report on Road Safety 2023* country profiles or its underlying harmonized estimates to compare deaths per 100,000 population and estimated totals for the same reference year. Use each national authority's data for local programme detail, while clearly labeling the source. Do not compare India's 2024 police count directly with Singapore's or Viet Nam's 2024 national count and call the difference a safety ranking.

For the intended India–Viet Nam–Singapore story, use a small dashboard with:

- estimated road deaths per 100,000 people (same WHO reference year);
- estimated deaths per 100,000 registered vehicles, where definitions allow;
- national reported fatalities, separately labeled with the national source and year;
- road-user mix and motorcycle exposure;
- population, motorization and urbanization context;
- whether the national definition uses a 30-day death window and how seriously injured people are counted.

Our World in Data's road-injury death chart is WHO-based and gives modeled annual death counts. Its 2021 display lists India at 205,894, Viet Nam at 17,229 and Singapore at a much smaller count. Counts mostly measure country size; use its per-capita data or normalize the series before suggesting relative individual risk. In addition, this OWID series and the WHO country profile may reflect different WHO releases or estimation vintages, so do not combine values without checking the metadata.

### 3.2 What the comparisons can and cannot show

- **India versus Viet Nam:** both have substantial motorcycle use, making helmet protection and enforcement relevant. Differences in population, travel patterns, road types, income, reporting and the date of law implementation still limit simple outcome comparisons.
- **India versus Singapore:** Singapore is useful for enforcement operations, data transparency, vehicle regulation and urban road management. Its small, highly urbanized setting is not a direct proxy for India's rural roads, informal transport or federal delivery structure.
- **Singapore versus Viet Nam:** use this as a comparison of different governance and implementation models, not a league table. Evaluate individual interventions and their context.
- **India versus Sweden or the Netherlands:** these countries can illustrate system design and institutional approaches. Their current outcomes reflect decades of policy, infrastructure, income and travel-mode differences; they are not achievable through one short-term measure.

StatsGeo pages in the supplied structure are general country-comparison pages, not verified road-safety datasets. Do not use them for road-fatality claims unless the indicator, source, year, units and definition are visible and validated against primary sources.

## 4. India's policy and delivery landscape

### 4.1 Legal and national framework

The Motor Vehicles Act and the 2019 amendment provide the statutory base for road-user duties, licensing, vehicle regulation and penalties. The amendment raised penalties for several offences and added provisions concerning road safety, electronic monitoring, vehicle recalls, Good Samaritans and a National Road Safety Board framework. Actual rules, enforcement and service delivery are shared across central and state institutions, police, transport departments, road-owning agencies, urban local bodies, health services and vehicle manufacturers.

The National Road Safety Policy (2010) sets a broad agenda around awareness, safer infrastructure, standards, enforcement, research and emergency care. MoRTH programmes include road-safety engineering and black-spot treatment, road-safety audits, highway patrol and incident response, driver training, vehicle standards, public-awareness activity, and the Good Samaritan framework. The future website should link to current official programme pages and distinguish a programme's existence from evidence that it reduced deaths.

### 4.2 Enforcement and reporting

The eChallan system supports electronic traffic enforcement and payment workflows. Its public reporting pages may offer challan activity, but a number of challans is an **output**, not a safety outcome. To evaluate deterrence, pair enforcement data with speed distributions, helmet and belt wearing, repeat violations, injury crashes, fatal crashes and exposure. Explain that detection totals may rise when enforcement improves even as risk later falls.

### 4.3 Implementation and data gaps

Key implementation questions for the report and future website:

- Are helmet and seat-belt rules enforced consistently, including for pillion riders and rear-seat occupants?
- Are speed limits credible for road design and enforced on high-risk corridors?
- Are black spots fixed with engineering changes, and are sites re-audited after treatment?
- Are road works, roadside hazards, unsafe junctions and pedestrian desire lines addressed before a crash occurs?
- Are state police and hospitals capturing a common crash identifier and linking incident, ambulance, hospital and death records?
- Are serious injuries and deaths after the crash tracked using a consistent time window?
- Do urban and rural local bodies have staff, funds and authority to deliver safe streets?
- Are crash investigations used to identify system failures, not only to assign individual blame?

## 5. What is known about policy effectiveness?

### 5.1 Motor Vehicles (Amendment) Act 2019

The statute has a plausible deterrence pathway through higher penalties and improved enforcement capacity. However, the law's nationwide implementation overlapped with changing mobility, COVID-19 restrictions, state-level variation and other road-safety actions. The Ministry told Parliament in March 2020 that it had not conducted a specific study to ascertain the Act's impact. A short before-and-after comparison is therefore not a credible causal estimate.

A 2021 autopsy-based study from one setting examined patterns before and after the amendment; its design and local sample limit national generalization. It can generate hypotheses, but cannot establish that the Act caused a national change in deaths. A stronger evaluation would use multiple years of state and district data, implementation dates and enforcement measures, comparison groups, traffic exposure and a pre-registered analysis plan.

### 5.2 Viet Nam's comprehensive helmet law

Viet Nam's nationwide helmet requirement for riders and passengers on all roads took effect on 15 December 2007, accompanied by a ten-fold penalty increase, intensive public communication and mobilized police enforcement. A study of patients admitted to 20 hospitals found a 16% reduction in head-injury risk and an 18% reduction in road-traffic death risk in the first three months after implementation. A related case study reports 680,000 infringements issued in 2008 and helmet wearing in Da Nang rising from 27% to 99% in the first six months.

This is encouraging evidence but has limits: short follow-up, a before-and-after design, selected hospitals and provinces, and simultaneous policy changes. The transferable lesson is the **implementation package**—clear coverage, credible sanctions, accessible helmets, communication and sustained enforcement—not that an identical short-term effect is guaranteed in India.

### 5.3 Singapore automated enforcement

Singapore's official 2024 road-traffic report documents automated and police enforcement activity alongside annual injury and fatality data. National evidence from a single year does not by itself isolate a camera effect. The supplied Singapore source describes a camera trial and plans; use it to explain operations and trial design, not as proof of a specific national fatality reduction unless the underlying evaluation reports a counterfactual outcome.

Singapore is a useful model for publishing violations and casualty indicators together. Its 2024 report also shows that despite enforcement, fatalities increased from 136 in 2023 to 142 in 2024 and speeding violations increased substantially. That is a reminder to avoid attributing all trends to enforcement counts alone and to monitor safety outcomes and exposure.

### 5.4 United Kingdom average-speed cameras

A UK parliamentary answer cites a 2016 evaluation of 50 average-speed-camera sites across Great Britain. After adjustment for regression to the mean and national casualty trends, the study found a 36.4% reduction in fatal and serious collisions and a 16% reduction in all injury collisions. This is stronger than a raw before-and-after count, though it remains an observational site evaluation in a different context. For India, pilot corridors should be selected transparently, compared with suitable control corridors and assessed for speed, collisions, deaths and displacement to nearby roads.

### 5.5 Reading results responsibly

Keep three evidence levels distinct:

1. **Measured implementation:** laws passed, cameras installed, challans issued, audits completed.
2. **Observed outcomes:** helmet wearing, speeds, crashes, injuries and deaths changed during a defined period.
3. **Causal effect:** credible comparison shows what changed because of the intervention, after accounting for other factors.

A policy can be implemented without being enforced; enforcement can increase without reducing risk; and outcomes can change for reasons unrelated to a policy. Label each claim accordingly.

## 6. International policies and adaptation for India

| Example | Core approach | Evidence or value | What India could adapt | Conditions and cautions |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Viet Nam | Universal motorcycle helmet law, stronger penalties, intensive public communication and enforcement | Hospital surveillance found short-term reductions in head injuries and deaths after the comprehensive law | Enforce helmet requirements for rider and pillion; improve access to certified helmets; focus on high-motorcycle-use districts | Ensure helmet quality, correct fastening, affordability and consistent state enforcement; measure wearing, not only challans |
| Singapore | Automated enforcement, transparent annual traffic reporting and structured police operations | Strong example of operational reporting; its own 2024 counts also show that violations and fatalities can rise together | Expand transparent speed-camera pilots on high-risk corridors; publish site-level speed and casualty outcomes | Privacy safeguards, due process, fair siting, maintenance and public explanation; camera counts are not impact estimates |
| Sweden | Vision Zero / Safe System: human error is expected; road design and speeds should prevent fatal or severe injury | Long-term national framework, shared accountability and interim targets | Adopt an India-wide severe-injury and death reduction target with state plans; prioritize safe speeds and forgiving roads | Needs stable accountability, funding, measurement and coordination; do not present the long-run outcome as a short project result |
| Netherlands | Sustainable Safety: road function, design and use should fit together; separate incompatible traffic where practical | A useful framework for functional road hierarchy and self-explaining roads | Classify roads by function; protect vulnerable users; redesign dangerous mixing at high-speed roads and settlements | Adapt to India's mixed traffic, informal access and varied road ownership; avoid copying geometry without local engineering |
| United Kingdom | Average-speed cameras assessed using comparison sites and adjusted analyses | Study of 50 sites reported 36.4% fewer fatal/serious collisions and 16% fewer injury collisions | Pilot average-speed enforcement on suitable expressways and corridors with baseline/control data | Transfer estimates cautiously; establish lawful data handling, signage, calibration and independent evaluation |
| Victoria, Australia | Graduated Driver Licensing (GDL): supervised learning and staged privileges for novice drivers | Reduces exposure to high-risk driving situations while experience develops; transport department provides an operational example | Consider staged licensing and risk limits for novice drivers, especially night/passenger exposure | Requires reliable licensing, supervision and enforcement; design must not make mobility inaccessible to rural or low-income learners |

### 6.1 Safe System adaptation principles

A practical Indian adaptation should treat a fatal crash as a system failure with several possible layers of protection:

- **Safe speeds:** set limits by road function and vulnerable-user presence; redesign roads so the operating speed matches the intended limit; use targeted automated enforcement.
- **Safe roads:** prioritize high-risk corridors and junctions; add medians, forgiving roadsides, safe crossings, footpaths, lighting and access management; protect work zones.
- **Safe vehicles:** strengthen crashworthiness and occupant protection, promote certified helmets, require functioning restraints, and regulate commercial vehicle safety.
- **Safe road users:** enforce impairment, speeding, helmet and restraint rules fairly; improve licensing and training; communicate risk without making education a substitute for safe design.
- **Post-crash response:** shorten discovery and ambulance response times, improve trauma pathways, and track serious injury and 30-day outcomes.
- **Shared accountability:** give a named public authority responsibility for each target, budget, milestone and annual public report.

## 7. A sequenced recommendation agenda for India

### Near term: next 12–24 months

1. **Publish a unified, versioned data dashboard.** Reconcile MoRTH, police, health and transport records where feasible; publish revisions, definitions, reporting coverage, 30-day fatality rules, serious-injury definitions and downloadable data.
2. **Target proven risk factors.** Prioritize speed management, universal helmet use including pillion riders, seat-belt use in all seats, impaired-driving enforcement and safe crossing access around schools, transit and markets.
3. **Treat high-risk locations.** Use transparent criteria to select black spots, junctions and corridors; publish before/after engineering audits and outcome measures.
4. **Set up independent evaluation.** Every major pilot should state its theory of change, baseline, comparison strategy, equity safeguards and outcome indicators before launch.
5. **Strengthen emergency response.** Map ambulance coverage and trauma capacity against high-fatality corridors; measure response times and survival, not just vehicles deployed.

### Medium term: 2–5 years

1. **Make safe speeds a network policy.** Define road classes, speed-limit rules and design standards that account for pedestrians and two-wheelers; scale proven camera systems with due process.
2. **Deliver safe-system street design.** Require safety audits for new construction and major upgrades, prioritize retrofits on existing roads, and fund footpaths and crossings as core road infrastructure.
3. **Improve vehicle and fleet safety.** Use procurement, inspection and fleet rules to lift safety for buses, trucks, taxis and commercial two-wheelers; publish compliance outcomes.
4. **Build state accountability.** Set state targets, annual budgets and district dashboards; link technical support to measurable delivery while accounting for exposure and reporting quality.
5. **Use novice-driver protections.** Pilot staged licensing and supervision where administrative capacity exists; evaluate effects and access barriers before broader rollout.

### Long term: 5–10 years

1. Embed a Vision Zero / Safe System principle in national and state transport planning: deaths and serious injuries are preventable and the system must account for human vulnerability.
2. Align land use, public transport, walking, cycling and road design to reduce exposure to high-speed conflict.
3. Continue longitudinal evaluation and update interventions as evidence and travel patterns change.

## 9. Definitions and caveats for publication

- **Road crash/accident:** use the source's term and definition; “crash” is preferred in editorial text because “accident” can imply inevitability.
- **Fatality:** state the time window after a crash. International systems often use a 30-day definition; verify India's administrative definition for each report.
- **Serious injury:** do not substitute “injured” or hospital admission for a clinically defined serious injury without explanation.
- **Rate:** always provide numerator, denominator, year and source. Population, registered vehicles and vehicle-kilometres answer different questions.
- **Reported versus estimated:** never place police-reported and WHO-estimated deaths in one line without clear separate labels.
- **Policy effectiveness:** a temporal decline after a law does not prove the law caused it. Describe study design and limitations.
- **Data revisions:** preserve archived versions and report publication date, data year and any revision date.

## Editorial recommendation

Build the public website around a simple claim supported by traceable evidence: **road deaths in India are preventable, but progress requires a coordinated system that protects people when predictable mistakes occur.** Put the 2024 MoRTH baseline up front, show WHO estimates separately, focus on vulnerable road users, and use international experience as tested ideas to adapt and evaluate—not as unqualified rankings or promises.
